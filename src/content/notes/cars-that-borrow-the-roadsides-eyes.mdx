A self-driving car can only see what its own sensors can see. A bus pulls into the next lane and everything behind it disappears. A sensor on a pole at the intersection has no such problem: it sits high, it doesn't move, and it sees the whole junction at once. Cooperative perception asks the obvious question: what if the car could borrow that view?

My undergraduate thesis looked at one part of that question. Once a car and a roadside unit agree to share, _what_ should they send each other?

## Three things you can send

Both sides start with LiDAR point clouds. There are three places to hand data over:

- **Early fusion** sends the raw point clouds. Nothing is lost, but it's heavy: the DAIR-V2X authors measured about 1.4 MB per frame.
- **Late fusion** sends only the finished detections, the boxes. That's about 336 bytes a frame, but each side has already thrown away everything its own model wasn't sure about.
- **Intermediate fusion** sends something in between: the feature maps a detector builds on its way to producing boxes. Richer than boxes, much smaller than raw points.

My bet was that intermediate fusion would beat both on accuracy, while costing far less bandwidth than early fusion.

## The data

DAIR-V2X was recorded at real intersections, with a car and a roadside unit capturing the same scenes from both sides: about 70,000 LiDAR point clouds and camera frames.[^real] Its labels cover cars, trucks, vans, buses, pedestrians, cyclists, tricyclists, motorcyclists and traffic cones.

## Plan A, and what happened to it

The plan was to add intermediate fusion to the DAIR-V2X authors' own code, which already did early and late fusion. It didn't go well. Every error I fixed surfaced another, and contacting the authors and filing issues on their GitHub didn't unblock it. Eventually I stopped.

Then, in March 2023, a new model called CoAlign came out with intermediate fusion for DAIR-V2X built in. It also fixes a problem I'd have hit anyway: the car and the roadside unit never agree perfectly on where they are relative to each other, so their feature maps don't line up. CoAlign builds a small graph of the agents and the objects they can both see, and solves for the poses that make those objects agree before it fuses anything.

## What the numbers said

I trained CoAlign's early, late and intermediate variants, all with a PointPillars encoder, and scored them on average precision.[^ap]

| fusion       | AP@0.5    | AP@0.7    |
| ------------ | --------- | --------- |
| early        | 0.729     | 0.596     |
| late         | 0.716     | 0.573     |
| intermediate | **0.772** | **0.627** |

Intermediate fusion won at both thresholds. The comparison with the DAIR-V2X authors' published baselines (0.537 early, 0.480 late) looks even better, but it isn't a fair one: their numbers came from a different split of the data, and CoAlign's version of the dataset adds extra labels for full 360° detection.

The bandwidth half of the bet I couldn't settle. The code only estimated bytes sent to an order of magnitude, which is too coarse to rank the three methods. That's the part I'd re-run first.
